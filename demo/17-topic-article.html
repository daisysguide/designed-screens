The Village
Who will we trust to watch the baby or babysit?
Dr. Daisy, MD
Medically reviewed · 3 min read
"Asking for help is never a sign of weakness. It's one of the bravest things you can do."
– Lily Collins
Why this matters
The first weeks (and months) after birth can be intense. You might find yourselves tag-teaming every diaper and nap, wondering how anyone does this with multiple kids or a job or both. And while many parents want to be there for every moment, there are times when rest, space, or outside obligations call for another set of trusted hands.
Start with your comfort zone
Ask yourselves: who would we feel comfortable leaving our baby with, even briefly? It might be:
  • A grandparent who lives nearby and has raised children of their own
  • A sibling or cousin who's great with kids and eager to help
  • A best friend who's basically family
  • No one yet, and that's okay
Talk through what makes each person a good fit (or not). Consider how they interact with children, how they respond to your parenting style, and how willing they are to follow your preferences around sleep, feeding, or screen time.
When are we actually comfortable stepping away?
Some parents are ready for a short break a week after birth. Others wait months, or longer, before letting someone else take over, even briefly. There's no universal timeline, but it helps to think about:
  • How you each feel about leaving the baby, even for an hour
  • What kind of breaks feel restorative (a walk? a nap? a date?)
  • Whether you'd prefer the sitter to come to your home or bring the baby to theirs
  • If you want someone to watch the baby solo, or just be an extra pair of hands while you're around
What support do we actually want?
Not all help looks the same. You might want someone who:
  • Can hold the baby while you shower or nap
  • Offers to come by once a week for a few hours so you can run errands
  • Feels confident managing bottles, diapers, or naps on their own
  • Is up for occasional evening babysitting
Talk about the kind of support that would actually help, not just check a box. Sometimes what you need most is someone to sit with you, not someone to hold the baby. And that's completely okay.
Think about situational support
Even if you don't plan to leave your baby with anyone right away, life has a way of throwing curveballs. A last-minute doctor's appointment, an unexpected illness, or even a long-overdue nap can create situations where having someone trusted to watch the baby, even briefly, makes a big difference. Talk about what kinds of scenarios might prompt you to ask for help:
  • Would you want someone nearby if you're recovering from a tough birth?
  • Do you want the flexibility to run to a medical appointment without the baby?
  • If you have older kids, do you need someone to juggle school pickups or bedtime?
You don't have to map out every possibility, but identifying a few people who could step in when needed can relieve stress. Even if they're not the go-to babysitter, they might be the right person to lean on in a pinch.
How do we talk to loved ones about this?
You may have people in your life who are eager to jump in and help, and that enthusiasm can be a real gift. Some parents are excited to welcome that support right away. Others prefer to settle into their new routine before inviting others in. You can be honest and appreciative, while still setting the pace that works best:
  • "We're still figuring out our rhythm, but we'd love your help when the timing feels right."
  • "We're so grateful you want to be involved. We may take it slow at first, but it means a lot to know you're there."
  • "We're easing into things for now, but we'd love for you to come by and meet the baby soon."
Setting kind, clear expectations doesn't mean shutting people out; it means making space for support in a way that honors your needs as new parents.
Trust takes time
If you're not ready to leave the baby with anyone yet, that's okay. You might find that your comfort level shifts as your baby grows, sleeps more consistently, or starts recognizing familiar faces. You can start small:
  • Have someone come by while you're home and gradually increase their involvement
  • Step out for 10 minutes to get coffee while your sitter watches the baby
  • Practice short solo outings and build from there
This slow build helps both you and your baby adjust, especially if you're feeling anxious about time apart.
What makes someone a trusted caregiver
Whoever watches your baby, even for an hour, should be someone you fully trust. Whether it's a grandparent, a close friend, or a sitter you're just getting to know, it helps to be thoughtful about who feels like the right fit for your family. A good caregiver is someone who:
  • Respects your rules and routines, even if they'd do things differently
  • Knows the basics of baby safety, like safe sleep, bottle handling, and spotting signs of distress
  • Is healthy, sober, and fully present
  • Feels comfortable asking questions and following instructions
Even with people you know and love, alignment matters. If you're exploring care from someone who hasn't spent much time around babies, or whose parenting style is very different from yours, that doesn't have to be a dealbreaker. But it might mean starting small: staying nearby during the first visit, writing down instructions, or doing a short trial run while you're home. If you'll be away from your baby for more than a short stretch, it's helpful to leave your caregiver with emergency contact numbers, your pediatrician's info, and any relevant medical or allergy details. And if something doesn't sit right? You're allowed to pause, pivot, or say no. This isn't about being polite; it's about protecting your baby and your peace of mind.
Revisiting the plan
What feels right in month one might look different in month six. Maybe your baby's grandparents get more comfortable with bottle-feeding, or a friend becomes your go-to for Friday afternoon walks. Maybe your priorities change, or your work schedule does, and you need to lean on someone new. Make space to check in about this every so often. Ask each other:
  • Is our current setup working?
  • Are we getting the rest and connection we need?
  • Are there people we want to involve, or step back from?
You're not handing off your role; you're building your village
Letting someone else care for your baby, even for a little while, doesn't mean you're stepping away. It means you're making space to rest, reset, and take care of yourself. With trust and clear communication, you can build a support system that feels good for everyone as you settle into this new chapter.
